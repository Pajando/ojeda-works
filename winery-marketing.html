Winery marketing: the honest playbook.

Every winery in the valley pours good wine in a pretty room — that's the table stakes, not the pitch. What sells is the one story competitors can't copy: your land, your family, your stubborn way of doing one thing. We're based in Napa; this is the conversation we hear every week.

Sell the story only you own

A visitor can taste at forty doors this weekend. The winery they remember is the one with a specific story — the block replanted after the fire, the third-generation pruning method, the winemaker who answers the tasting-room questions personally. Put that story in plain text on your site, in your Google Business Profile description, and in the first thirty seconds of every video. “Award-winning wines in the heart of wine country” describes four hundred wineries; it markets none of them.

The club is the engine, tastings are the doorway

Tasting fees keep the lights on; the club pays for the harvest. Every marketing decision should bend toward one question: does this bring the right visitor to the bar, and does the experience earn the signup? That means booking links that work, honest photography of what a visit actually feels like, and follow-up emails written like a person — not a drip campaign. A club member is worth years of revenue; treat the ask with that respect.

The foundation and what to skip

Complete Google Business Profile — accurate hours (harvest season included), reservation link, real photos by the dozen, reviews answered like a human. A site that says in readable text where you are, what a visit costs, and whether kids or dogs can come — the exact questions people ask AI assistants planning a trip. What to skip: paying for broad “wine country” ad keywords you can't win, and posting bottle-on-a-barrel photos daily. One honest video of your vineyard at 6am outworks a month of stock shots.

Questions people actually ask

How do wineries attract more visitors?

Be findable and bookable at the moment someone plans the trip: a complete profile with reservation link, real photos, current hours, and reviews. Then give travel planners — human and AI — a specific reason to pick you over the door next to you: the story, the view, the format of the visit, in plain text they can read.

What is the best marketing for a small winery?

The wine club, worked honestly. Tastings and events exist to earn signups; email keeps members close; the story keeps them proud to belong. Paid ads come last, pointed only at a booking page that already converts.

Should a winery be on social media?

Yes, but as a window, not a megaphone: harvest, bottling, the dog in the vineyard, the people. Two to four real posts a week beats daily promotion. The goal is that a future visitor feels they already know the place.

Do wineries need a website if they have a tasting room?

Yes — trip planning happens online before anyone drives up the hill. Hours, fees, formats, directions, and the reservation link in machine-readable text decide whether search engines and AI assistants send visitors to you or past you.
