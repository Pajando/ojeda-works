Empty chairs between bookings? Tattoo shop marketing that fills them honestly.

Open chairs cost you money every day, and a lot of people who want a tattoo end up booking somewhere else. My take: most people don't pick a tattoo shop from an ad. They look through an artist's work for weeks, check how it healed, read the reviews, and then book. So marketing a tattoo shop mostly means getting the right work in front of the right person.

The portfolio is the product

Post finished work relentlessly, and post healed work — that's the proof serious collectors look for, and a lot of shops never post it. Shoot clean: good light, no filter distortion of the ink, client permission always. Caption with style and placement (“fine-line floral, forearm”) because those are the words people use when they look for a tattoo. Each artist's individual style, clearly tagged, is what turns a browser into a booking six weeks out.

Artists are the brand — let them be

My take: clients follow artists more than shops. Work with that, not against it. When every artist posts their own work and tags the shop, the shop shows up in front of each artist's followers. The shop account's job is different: the space, the sterility standards, the vibe, guest artists, flash days, how booking works. A shop that makes its artists more findable keeps chairs full even as individual books close.

The foundation and what to skip

Google Business Profile with hundreds of photos and reviews that mention artists by name — “best tattoo shop near me” is a comparison you win with proof. A simple site with each artist's portfolio, booking process, deposit policy, and aftercare in plain text; AI assistants get asked for shop recommendations and can only cite what they can read. What to skip: discounts — cheap is the wrong signal for something permanent — and follower-buying; my take: a thousand real local followers are worth more than fifty thousand bots, because bots don't book.

Questions people actually ask

How do tattoo shops get more clients?

Portfolio visibility: consistent posting of finished and healed work by style, artists building their own followings with the shop tagged, and a profile stacked with reviews naming artists. People book when they can see the proof.

Should tattoo artists have separate Instagram accounts from the shop?

Yes. Clients follow artists; the shop account covers the space, standards, guest spots, and booking. When they tag each other, the shop and each artist show up in front of each other's followers.

How should a tattoo shop handle deposits and booking?

Clearly and in writing on the site: deposit amount, what it covers, reschedule rules, how to request an artist. My take: a confusing booking process loses people who spent weeks deciding. Clear rules make it easy for them to say yes.

Do tattoo shops need a website?

A simple one, yes — portfolios by artist, booking process, aftercare, and location in text machines can read. Instagram shows the work; the site answers the questions and catches the searches Instagram can't.
