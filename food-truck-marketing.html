Fans can't find your truck today? Food truck marketing starts with where you're parked.

Your restaurant moves. So the fan who loved your tacos last week has to guess where you are today, and if they guess wrong, you lose the sale. My take: if people can't find you today, nothing else matters. Here's the playbook.

Broadcast the location like it's the product

Because it is. Put today's spot and hours everywhere, every day: your social bio, a morning story, an update on your Google Business Profile, and a simple site with the weekly schedule as real text. Google lets you post updates that show on your profile in Search and Maps (Google Business Profile Help). My take: treat the schedule like a menu item. A smaller crowd that always knows where you are beats a big following that has to guess.

Appetite content, shot at the window

You make new content every shift: the steam, the flip, the cheese pull, the line. Shoot short phone clips during service and put today's location in the caption. My take: post before people decide where to eat, late morning for lunch and late afternoon for dinner. One real clip of a line at the window beats a month of graphics.

The bookings side: where slow weeks get covered

My take: catering and private events are where the better money is: office lunches, weddings, brewery nights. Give them a page of their own with real photos, what's included, and an easy way to ask for a date. People planning events search online too. If “food truck catering [your area]” turns up a clear page with proof, you have a shot at bookings that street parking won't bring.

Quick answers

How do food trucks announce their location?

Everywhere, more than once: a morning social post, an updated bio, a Google profile update, and a weekly schedule page on a simple site. Fans read the stories. Search engines and AI tools read the site and the profile. My take: if your schedule only lives in stories, a search for “food trucks near me today” has little to show.

What social media works best for food trucks?

My take: Instagram and TikTok for food clips, posted before mealtimes. But the real answer is wherever your customers already look, so ask the people in line. One app posted daily with real food beats three apps updated weekly with graphics.

How do food trucks get catering gigs?

A catering page with photos, packages and a fast way to ask for a date. Then ask for a review after every event, while people are still happy. Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking (Google Business Profile Help). My take: one good private booking can cover a slow week on the street.

Do food trucks need a website?

My take: yes, a simple one. Schedule, menu as text, catering page, contact. It gives Google and AI tools something clear to read about you, and it's yours. A social account's reach can drop when the app changes how it shows posts. A few fast pages are enough for a truck.
