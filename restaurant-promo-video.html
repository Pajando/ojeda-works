Video

A restaurant promo video that brings diners in, not just views.

The best restaurant videos don't look like commercials — they look like proof: real food coming out of a real kitchen, real people eating it. Here's what works, what it commonly costs, and the ideas worth stealing.

Ideas that outperform polish

The dish-build shot — one signature dish assembled start to finish, cut fast. The kitchen-at-rush shot — motion, steam, and noise sell more than any slogan. The regular's-order shot — a real customer, their usual, one sentence about why. And the owner saying one true thing about why the place exists — thirty seconds, no script-speak.

What underperforms: drone shots of the building, stock-feeling slow motion, and menus read aloud. People choose restaurants on appetite and trust; every second should feed one of the two.

What it commonly costs

A short promo from a solo videographer commonly runs a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars depending on shoot time and editing. Social-cut packages — one shoot turned into several platform-ready clips — commonly deliver the most value per dollar, because the same kitchen footage feeds a month of posts.

AI-assisted production can stretch this further for concept pieces and menu highlights, but the core of restaurant video is real food and real people — that part can't be generated, and diners can tell.

Where the video should live

Not just Instagram. Your Google Business Profile accepts video, and it shows up right where hungry people are deciding — the map results. Your website should carry it too, on a page that says what you serve and where, in text machines can read. A great video on a page Google can't understand is a billboard in a basement.

Questions people actually ask

How long should a restaurant promo video be?

For social feeds, commonly 15–30 seconds — appetite is instant or not at all. For your website or Google Business Profile, up to about a minute works. Save anything longer for a story worth telling, like a founding-family piece; nobody watches a three-minute menu.

Can I just film my restaurant with a phone?

For day-to-day social content, absolutely — real and current beats polished and stale. Where paid production earns its cost is the anchor piece: the one well-shot, well-edited video that lives on your site and profile for a year and sets the tone everything else borrows.

Do restaurant videos actually bring in customers?

Video of real food is among the most persuasive content a restaurant can publish, because it answers the only two questions that matter: does this look good, and does this place feel right. The mechanism is simple — appetite plus trust. Generic footage that answers neither is why some videos do nothing.

What's the biggest restaurant video mistake?

Making it about the building instead of the food and the people. The second biggest: posting it once on one platform and letting it die. One shoot should become an anchor video plus a month of clips, spread across the profile, the site, and social.
