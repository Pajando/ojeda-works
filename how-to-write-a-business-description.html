How to write a business description — for Google and everywhere else.

Somewhere right now a customer is reading “Welcome to our website, your one-stop shop for all your needs” and learning nothing. The business description is the most-copied, least-thought-about text a business owns — and it's what humans skim, search engines index, and AI assistants quote when they describe you. Here's the formula, and the official rules for the Google version.

The formula: four answers in plain words

Every strong business description answers four things fast: what you do (in the words a customer would use, not your industry's), who it's for, where you serve, and why you — the one honest, specific thing that makes you the pick. “Bilingual auto repair in Napa — we photograph the worn part and explain the fix before we charge you” beats three paragraphs of “commitment to excellence,” because every word is checkable and specific to the business. Write it once, deploy it consistently everywhere — website, Google profile, directories, social bios — because that consistency is exactly what machines cross-check when deciding whether to recommend you.

Google's actual rules for the profile description

Google's guidelines for the Business Profile description are short and mostly ignored: be upfront and honest, keep it relevant and useful to customers, no links of any kind, and don't make it a promo board — sale prices and “best in town for $5!” pitches are explicitly against the rules, as are gimmicky characters and gibberish. What belongs there, per Google: your services, your story, what makes you distinct. Most profiles leave the field empty or stuff it with keywords; a plain, honest paragraph following the four-answer formula puts you ahead of both.

The mistakes that make descriptions invisible

Three failure modes cover almost every bad description. The mirror problem: written about yourself instead of for the reader (“founded in 2009, we pride ourselves on…” — nobody searched for your pride). The everything problem: listing every service you've ever performed until nothing stands out — a description that claims everything positions nothing. The jargon problem: your industry's words instead of your customer's (“multi-channel fenestration solutions” for a window company). The test for all three: read it aloud and ask if a stranger could repeat back what you do, for whom, where, and why you. If they can't, neither can the AI assistant summarizing you to a customer tonight.

Questions people actually ask

What should a business description include?

Four answers: what you do in customer language, who you serve, where, and the one specific honest reason to pick you. Skip slogans, awards nobody's heard of, and adjectives you can't prove — specificity is what both readers and machines keep.

How long should a business description be?

Short enough to survive skimming: a tight paragraph leads, and the strongest sentence goes first — profiles and search results truncate, so the first line has to work alone. Say the four answers, then stop.

Can I put my prices or website link in my Google description?

No links — Google's guidelines prohibit them in the description field — and promo-style price pitches are against the rules too. Prices belong on your site and services; the description is for what you do, whom you serve, and why you.

Should my description be the same everywhere?

The facts, yes — identical name, services, and area everywhere, because consistency is what search engines and AI assistants verify you by. Length can flex per platform; the story shouldn't change with the venue.
