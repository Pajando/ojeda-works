Video

Not sure a video quote is fair? Here's how videographers charge.

You got a video quote and can't tell if it's fair. Or you're worried about paying for a video that brings in nobody. Here's how videographers charge, what to ask before you pay, and when you don't need one at all.

The three ways videographers charge

Hourly means you pay for time. My take: rates vary a lot by experience and town. For a baseline, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics says the median wage for camera operators was $74,990 a year in May 2025. That's what employees earn, not what a freelancer charges you. My take: a freelancer's rate also has to cover gear, software, and hours nobody pays for, so expect it to be higher. The shoot is often the smaller part, too, since editing can take longer, and it's billable.

Day rates are one price for a day of shooting. Always ask if that covers the shoot only or the editing too. If the quote doesn't say, treat editing as extra until you get it in writing.

Per-project means one price for a finished, delivered video. My take: it's the clearest deal for a business, because you know the total before anyone picks up a camera. Prices swing a lot between one person with a camera and a full production company, so get two or three quotes for the same video.

What actually drives the price

My take: gear matters less than it used to. A modern phone shoots usable footage. What you're really paying for is judgment: knowing what the video needs to say, planning the shots before the camera rolls, and cutting it so people actually watch to the end. A beautiful video with no point costs the same to make as one that brings in customers.

My take: location, travel, extra crew, licensed music, and revision rounds all add to the price, and “unlimited revisions” is often already priced into a higher quote. Get the revision policy in writing.

When you don't need a videographer

My take: not every video needs a camera crew anymore. AI-made video can work for some marketing pieces, like short social ads, product explainers, and concept pieces, and it can cost less than a shoot. It's the wrong tool for anything that needs to show your real face, your real shop, or real customers. A good video partner will tell you which is which instead of selling you the expensive option by default.

Questions people actually ask

Why is video editing so expensive?

My take: editing is most of the work. Someone has to watch everything that was shot, pick the best parts, then cut, color, and mix it into a few minutes people will actually sit through. That's skilled labor, and it often takes longer than the shoot itself.

Can I just make videos with my phone?

For some things, yes. My take: a modern phone shoots good footage, and real, unpolished clips can hold their own on social. Where it falls apart is planning and editing: knowing what to say, what to shoot, and what to cut. That judgment is what you're actually hiring.

Is AI-generated video good enough for marketing?

My take: for short social ads, explainers, and concept pieces, often yes, and it can cost less than a shoot. For anything that needs your real face, your real location, or real customers on camera, no. The right answer is usually a mix, chosen per project instead of one-size-fits-all.

What should I ask before hiring a videographer?

Three things. What's the total price for a finished, delivered video, not just the shoot day? How many rounds of changes are included? And who owns the footage afterward? The answers tell you a lot about who you're hiring.
