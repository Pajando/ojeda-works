HVAC marketing: keep the phone ringing between heat waves.

Slammed in the first heat wave, dead quiet in between? The calls you get in July are decided in spring. Here's the honest playbook.

Own the peaks (they're decided in advance)

When the AC dies in July, the homeowner searches right then. The call often goes to the company whose profile was already complete: recent reviews, honest hours, someone picking up the phone. Ads on urgent searches can pay in the peak even at high click prices, but the free way to show up in those searches comes from work done in spring. You can't fix your profile during the heat wave. That's when the spring work pays off.

Market the valleys with maintenance

My take: maintenance agreements are the steadiest part of an HVAC business. Spring AC tune-ups and fall furnace checks bring money in during the slow months, can head off breakdowns, and make you the company the household already knows. Market them in spring and fall: email past customers, put up a plain page that says what a tune-up includes, and show honest prices. What you're building is customers who call you first when something breaks.

Trust and the big-ticket quote

System replacements are big, researched purchases. What helps win them: plain pages on options and honest price ranges, financing explained simply, photos of clean installs, and reviews that name the job (“replaced our furnace in [town], showed us the old part”). Plenty of homeowners worry about being upsold. My take: the company that explains without pressure wins the shopper the pushy one loses.

No license number on your ad? A first offense can cost $100 to $1,000.

Here's the problem: in California, the ad itself has rules. The CSLB's license class for HVAC is the C-20, "Warm-Air Heating, Ventilating and Air-Conditioning Contractor." The board's own advertising fact sheet says:

  • Your license number goes on all advertising. That includes "any website or post that solicits business on the Internet." The civil penalty for a first offense is between $100 and $1,000.
  • Only advertise work your license covers. Selling work outside your classification in an ad is not allowed.
  • Don't say "bonded" in ads. The law bars it, because customers may think a bond protects them more than it does.
  • Trucks count. Business name and license number on each commercially registered vehicle, in a clearly visible location, in letters at least ¾ inch high and wide (or 72-point type).

The good news: a license number is free proof. A homeowner can type it into the CSLB's License Check in a minute. Put it in your website footer, your Google profile and every post. Same for refrigerant: the EPA says technicians who "maintain, service, repair, or dispose of equipment" that "could release ozone depleting refrigerants into the atmosphere must be certified." If your techs hold that certification, say so on the page.

Sources: CSLB, "Advertising Guidelines For Contractors" (Fast Facts, PDF) · CSLB license classifications · EPA, Section 608 Technician Certification. Checked October 23, 2026. This is not legal advice; the CSLB is the place to confirm.

The heat-wave checklist: finish it in spring

You can't fix your profile while the phone is ringing off the hook. Do these while it's quiet:

  1. Fix your Google service area. If customers don't come to your shop, Google says to remove the address from your profile. You can list up to 20 service areas, and the whole area shouldn't be more than about 2 hours of driving from your base.
  2. Make the hours honest. If you answer emergency calls at night, say so. If you don't, don't pretend.
  3. One page per job people search: AC repair, furnace repair, tune-up, system replacement. Each one says what's included and a price or a "from" price.
  4. Write down what a tune-up includes. A plain list beats "full inspection." It's also how you sell maintenance agreements without sounding like a subscription trap.
  5. Ask every finished job for a review that names the job and the town. Here's how to ask without being weird about it.
  6. License number everywhere: site footer, trucks, ads, Google posts (see above).
  7. Email past customers twice a year: AC tune-up in spring, furnace check in fall.
  8. Track one number for paid ads: cost per booked job, not cost per click.

And one thing to ignore: anyone who promises you the top spot. In Google's own words: "There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google." (Google Business Profile Help)

Source for the service-area rules: Google Business Profile Help, "Manage your service areas for service-area & hybrid businesses". Checked October 23, 2026.

My take: a Napa or Solano shop

This is my opinion, not data. Imagine a two-truck HVAC shop based in Vacaville that also takes jobs in Fairfield, Napa and American Canyon. (It's an example, not a real business.) Here's what I'd do in March, before anyone's AC dies:

  • List those four cities as service areas, and nothing they don't really drive to.
  • Add a short line per city on the site only where there are real jobs and real photos from there. No copy-paste city pages.
  • Send the spring tune-up email to every past customer, with the plain tune-up list and the price.
  • Put the C-20 license number in the footer, on the profile and on both trucks.
  • Ask the next 10 finished jobs for a review that names the town and the job.

Why bother with the website part at all? When we checked 257 local businesses in Napa and Solano counties, 52 had no website, and our scanner couldn't open 37 more. That scan wasn't HVAC-only, but the lesson carries: if Google and AI tools can't read what you do and where, the hot-July answer talks about someone else. See what we found.

Questions people actually ask

Do HVAC contractors in California have to put their license number in ads?

Yes. The Contractors State License Board's advertising fact sheet says licensed contractors must include their license number in all forms of advertising, and it counts any website or post that solicits business on the Internet as advertising. The civil penalty for a first offense is between $100 and $1,000.

When should HVAC companies advertise?

In spring and fall, work on your Google profile, your reviews and your maintenance emails. In the peaks, run ads on urgent searches like AC repair, with a budget cap, and track the cost of each booked job. If you only spend in July, you're late. The work that gets July calls happens in spring.

Are maintenance agreements worth promoting?

My take: they're the best thing you can promote. They bring steady monthly money, fill the slow seasons, and give you customers who call you first when something breaks. Price them honestly and show the work. An agreement that feels like a subscription trap gets canceled. One that prevents a breakdown is easy to renew.

How do HVAC companies get replacement leads?

By earning trust before the visit: pages that explain options and real cost ranges, clean install photos, reviews from named towns, and no-pressure quotes. Replacement shoppers compare companies; the one whose site already answered their questions starts ahead.

Do HVAC ads cost too much in summer?

My take: clicks often cost more in a heat wave, because more homeowners search and more companies bid on the same words. Ads can still pay if you keep them to your service area and track the cost of each booked job. Reviews and a complete Google profile help you show up in those same searches without paying per click, and that work doesn't stop when you stop paying. Use ads on top of it, not instead of it.
