Website Development

Paying a monthly website fee and not sure what it buys? Website maintenance cost, explained.

My take: maintenance quotes for the same small site can run from nothing to hundreds of dollars a month. That's because “maintenance” mixes real costs with fees that don't do much. Here's how to tell them apart.

The legitimate costs

Every website has a few real recurring costs. The domain name is a yearly fee (my estimate: around $10 to $20 for a .com; Cloudflare says it does not mark up domain prices). Hosting can be free (Cloudflare Pages has a Free plan) or cost more each month for bigger setups. Then there are content updates when your prices, hours, or services change. Those are real and worth paying for.

On sites built with plugins, like most WordPress sites, there's a second real cost: software updates and security fixes. WordPress's own security guide lists old, outdated plugins among the most common attacks and says to keep plugins updated. My estimate: paid plans for this often run about $30 to $150 or more a month, and on those platforms it isn't optional.

The rent dressed as service

The tell is a monthly fee that can't name what it does: “monitoring,” “optimization,” “support” with no log of actions taken. Ask any maintenance provider for last month's list of what they actually did to your site. A real provider has one; a rent collector has a subscription.

How static sites make most of this disappear

A static site (plain, fast pages with no database and no plugins) has very little to maintain: no plugins to patch, and hosting that can be free. The monthly cost drops to the domain and real content changes. My take: for most small businesses that's the better deal, and it's how I build sites.

Questions people actually ask

How much should website maintenance cost per month?

My estimate: for plugin-based sites like most WordPress sites, about $30 to $150 or more a month for real updates and security work. For static sites, close to zero: a yearly domain renewal plus content edits when something changes. If you pay monthly, you should be able to see exactly what the money did.

What happens if I don't maintain my WordPress site?

WordPress's own security guide lists outdated plugins among the most common ways sites get attacked. A hacked site can be defaced or send your visitors somewhere else. On plugin sites, maintenance really matters. My take: the way out isn't skipping it; it's moving to a simpler site that doesn't need it.

Can I do website maintenance myself?

Content updates, yes. Most platforms make text and photo edits easy enough. Security updates and plugin conflicts are where owners get burned, because a bad update can take the site down at 9pm while customers are trying to reach you. Know which half you're signing up for.

Is a website maintenance plan worth it?

Worth it when it covers real software upkeep on a plugin site, or content work you'd otherwise skip. Not worth it when it's a monthly fee with no list of what was done, on a site that rarely changes. My take: over five years, rebuilding as a simple static site often costs less than paying for maintenance forever.
