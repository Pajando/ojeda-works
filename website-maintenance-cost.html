Website maintenance cost: what's real, what's rent.

Maintenance quotes commonly run from nothing to hundreds a month for the same size site — because “maintenance” bundles legitimate costs with rent dressed up as service. Here's how to tell them apart.

The legitimate costs

Every website carries a few real recurring costs: the domain (commonly $10–$20 a year), hosting (free to $50+ a month depending on the setup), and content updates when your prices, hours, or services change. Those are real and worth paying for.

On platform-built sites — WordPress with plugins especially — there's a second legitimate layer: software updates and security patching, because outdated plugins are how sites get hacked. Commonly $30–$150+ a month, and on those platforms it genuinely isn't optional.

The rent dressed as service

The tell is a monthly fee that can't name what it does: “monitoring,” “optimization,” “support” with no log of actions taken. Ask any maintenance provider for last month's list of what they actually did to your site. A real provider has one; a rent collector has a subscription.

How static sites make most of this disappear

A static site — plain fast pages, no database, no plugin stack — has almost nothing to maintain: no software to patch, nothing to hack through, hosting that can be free. The recurring cost drops to the domain and actual content changes. That's not a compromise; for most small businesses it's strictly better, and it's why sites here are built that way.

Questions people actually ask

How much should website maintenance cost per month?

For plugin-based platform sites, commonly $30–$150+ for real updates and security work. For static sites, close to zero — a domain renewal yearly and content edits when something changes. If you're paying monthly, you should be able to see exactly what the money did.

What happens if I don't maintain my WordPress site?

Outdated plugins and themes are the most common way small-business sites get compromised — defaced, redirected, or blacklisted by Google. On plugin platforms, maintenance is genuinely load-bearing. The alternative isn't skipping it; it's moving to a foundation that doesn't need it.

Can I do website maintenance myself?

Content updates, yes — most platforms make text and image edits manageable. Security patching and plugin conflicts are where owners commonly get burned, because a failed update can take the site down at 9pm with customers watching. Know which half you're signing up for.

Is a website maintenance plan worth it?

Worth it when it covers real software upkeep on a plugin platform or genuine content work you'd otherwise neglect. Not worth it when it's an unnamed-services subscription on a site that rarely changes. The five-year math often favors rebuilding static over renting maintenance forever.
