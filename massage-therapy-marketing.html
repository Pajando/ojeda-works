Massage therapy marketing: the honest playbook.

Massage clients are buying two things: relief and safety. The therapist who communicates both — clear specialization, professional boundaries, easy booking — stays booked while better-skilled competitors with vague marketing sit idle.

Specialize in public

“Massage therapist” is a commodity search; “sports massage for runners,” “prenatal certified,” “post-surgical lymphatic drainage” are decisions already half-made. State your modalities, training, and who you're best for in plain text on your profile and site. Credentials matter here — licensure, certifications, continuing education — and so does the safety signal: professional space, clear policies, reviews that mention feeling comfortable. That's what nervous first-timers are scanning for.

The rebooking rhythm is the practice

Relief fades on a schedule; the therapists who thrive book the next session at the table (“same time in three weeks?”) and hold standing slots for regulars. Packages formalize the rhythm and smooth income. A practice with 70% rebooking needs a trickle of new clients, not a firehose — which frees marketing time for the referral sources that fit your niche: chiropractors, PTs, trainers, OB offices for prenatal work. One professional relationship refers more than a year of posts.

The foundation and what to skip

Google Business Profile complete — modalities, prices, booking link, photos of the actual space (the room sells safety), reviews answered gratefully. A simple site with rates, policies, and what-to-expect for first-timers in readable text; “massage near me prices” and “what should I expect at my first massage” are asked to search engines and AI assistants constantly. What to skip: discount-platform dependency (Groupon fills the table at a loss with serial deal-hunters), and vague wellness-speak — “holistic healing journey” loses to “I fix desk-job necks” in every search ever run.

Questions people actually ask

How do massage therapists get more clients?

Specialization stated plainly, professional referral relationships (chiro, PT, trainers), and a complete profile with the space visible and policies clear. The safety-and-specifics combination converts the hesitant majority.

How do massage therapists keep clients rebooking?

Book at the table, on the relief cycle — three or four weeks for most maintenance clients. Standing appointments for regulars, packages for structure, a reminder when the cycle lapses. Retention is rhythm, not luck.

Should massage therapists use Groupon?

Rarely, and never as the main pipeline — deep-discount buyers mostly chase the next deal, and the economics hurt. A paid intro rate you control converts better and attracts clients who can afford to return.

What should a massage therapist post on social media?

Educational and calm: stretch tips, what different modalities actually do, the room, policies that make first-timers comfortable. Frequency matters less than tone — the feed should feel like the appointment.
