Empty slots on the books? Massage therapy marketing that fills the table, honestly.

You're good with your hands, and you still have empty slots on Tuesday. My take: a new client is looking for two things, relief and feeling safe. If your profile and site don't show both clearly, they book someone who does. Here's how to show it.

Specialize in public

“Massage therapist” sounds like everyone else. “Sports massage for runners” or “prenatal massage” tells a person you're the one for them. Write down, in plain words on your profile and site, the kinds of massage you do, your training, and who you help most. Show your license and certifications. Then show the safety side: photos of the real room, clear policies, and reviews where clients say they felt comfortable. My take: that's what a nervous first-timer looks for before they book.

The rebooking rhythm is the practice

Relief wears off. So book the next visit before they leave the table (“Same time in three weeks?”) and hold standing slots for your regulars. Packages can help clients keep the habit and can make your income steadier. The more clients rebook, the fewer new ones you have to chase. Spend that free time on people who can send you clients that fit your work: chiropractors, physical therapists, trainers, and OB offices if you do prenatal. My take: one good referral partner can be worth more than a lot of posts.

The foundation and what to skip

Fill out your Google Business Profile completely: the kinds of massage you do, prices, booking link, photos of the real room, and a thank-you reply to every review. Google says businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results (Google Business Profile Help). Then build a simple site with your rates, your policies, and what a first visit is like, written as text, not a picture. Google's guide to its AI search features says they need no special optimization, and it lists putting important content in text form among the basics (Google Search Central). What to skip: living on deep-discount sites, and vague wellness talk. My take: “I fix desk-job necks” says more than “holistic healing journey.”

Questions people actually ask

How do massage therapists get more clients?

Say plainly who you help and what kind of massage you do. Build referral friendships with chiropractors, physical therapists and trainers. Keep your Google profile complete, with photos of the real room and clear policies. My take: a nervous first-timer books the therapist who looks both skilled and safe.

How do massage therapists keep clients rebooking?

Book the next visit before they leave, at whatever gap you and the client agree on. Give regulars a standing slot, offer packages, and send a reminder when someone is overdue. Keeping clients is a habit you build, not luck.

Should massage therapists use Groupon?

My take: rarely, and never as your main source of clients. Many deep-discount buyers move on to the next deal, and the price cut comes out of your pay. An intro price you set yourself is easier to control, and it draws people who can afford to come back.

What should a massage therapist post on social media?

Calm and useful: stretch tips, what each kind of massage actually does, your real room, and the policies that help first-timers relax. My take: how your feed feels matters more than how often you post. It should feel like the appointment.
