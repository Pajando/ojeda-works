Advertising

Worried Facebook ads will eat your money and bring no customers? Here's what they really cost.

You pick the budget. Meta even calls it “your most valuable cost control tool.” The scary part is that you can spend all of it and still not get one new customer. The real question is what one customer costs you, and the budget box doesn't show that. Here's the honest math.

How the pricing actually works

There's no fixed price. Each time your ad could show to someone, Meta runs an auction, and Meta says it is not like auctions “that pick a winner based on the highest bid.” You set a daily budget or a lifetime budget. On its pricing page, Meta says “Your ad campaign's objective plays a large role in impacting the cost of your ads,” and it lists ad quality, bidding strategy and performance goal as cost factors too. My take: anyone who quotes you one “average cost per click” for every business is guessing at your number.

One thing to know about daily budgets: Meta says it “may spend up to 75% over your daily promotional budget” on some days, but over a week “your spend will not be greater than seven times your daily budget.”

My take: start with a small test, a few hundred dollars over a few weeks, to learn one number: what a lead or a sale costs you. That's the only number that matters. Made-up example: if you spend $300 and get 6 calls, each call cost you $50.

What actually drives your cost down

Meta says its auction looks at ad quality, and that “Ad quality is determined from several sources, including assessments of low-quality attributes in the ad”. My take: the ad itself is the cheapest thing to fix. Use one clear, true offer with a real photo of your work. Then fix the page the ad sends people to. If they click and leave, you paid for a visit that bought nothing.

My take: timing matters too. If you can, run your test in a normal month, not the weeks before a big holiday when lots of stores are buying ads.

The mistake that wastes ad money

My take: it's running ads before the basics are ready. If your ad sends people to a half-done Google profile or a slow, confusing website, you're paying for a leak. People click, see nothing that convinces them, and leave. Do it in this order: finish your free listings, fix the page the ad lands on, pick one real offer, then spend.

How I'd start

My take: start small, watch one number (what a lead or a sale costs you), and stop an ad fast when it isn't working. Check the basics first. Can people find you? Does the page the ad sends them to make them want to call? Paying for ads that point to a broken page just burns your money.

Questions people actually ask

What's the minimum I can spend on Facebook ads?

On its ads pricing page, Meta says: “The recommended practice is to start with at least $5 for your budget and to choose a duration over six days.” My take: that's a floor, not a plan. The real minimum is enough to learn what one lead costs you, often a few hundred dollars over a few weeks. Spending $30 total teaches you almost nothing.

Are Facebook ads worth it for a small business?

My take: they can be, if you have a specific offer, a landing page that turns visits into calls, and someone tracking what each new customer costs. They're usually a waste when the ad says “check us out” and points at a homepage. The offer and the basics decide it, not the platform.

Why are my Facebook ads so expensive?

My take: usually one of three reasons. The ad doesn't grab people (Meta says ad quality is part of how its auction decides), lots of other advertisers want the same people you do, or you're running in a busy season against big budgets. Fix the ad first. It's usually the cheapest fix.

Should I run Facebook ads or Google ads?

My take: Google search ads reach people who are searching for what you do right now. Facebook ads mostly reach people by interest and location who weren't searching. So if your customers need you fast (a plumber, a tow truck), start with Google. If you have something people want to look at (food, hair, cars), start with Facebook. Ask your last ten customers where they found you. Their answer beats any rule.
