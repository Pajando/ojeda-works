How to get customer testimonials — legally, and worth reading.

Reviews live on Google; testimonials live where you put them — the website, the proposal, the quote email. Most businesses have neither, for the same reason: nobody asks at the right moment. Here's the asking system, the federal rules almost nobody knows exist, and the difference between a testimonial that converts and a wall of praise nobody believes.

The ask is a system, not a favor

The moment is identical to the review ask — the peak, when the work just delivered — but the request is different: “could I quote you on that?” When a customer says something specific in an email or text (“honestly the cleanest job site we've ever had”), ask permission to use exactly those words with their first name and town. That's the entire system: catch real praise in the wild, ask to keep it. Interviews work too — three questions by phone (“what was the problem, what happened, what surprised you”) produce testimonials no one could invent, in a voice no marketer can fake.

The rules: the FTC is not kidding

The FTC's endorsement guides set the federal rules: testimonials must reflect the endorser's real experience; if you feature an exceptional result, you have to make clear what customers generally get; and any material connection — a discount, a gift, a friendship the reader wouldn't expect — must be disclosed. Fabricated testimonials and invented customers are straightforwardly illegal, and enforcement is real. None of this is burdensome for an honest business: use real words from real customers, show typical results, disclose anything traded. The rules only hurt businesses that were faking it.

Where testimonials actually convert

Not on a “Testimonials” page — nobody visits it. Put them next to the decision they support: the roofing quote goes out with two roofing testimonials; the service page carries the testimonial about that service; the proposal's pricing page carries the one that says it was worth it. Specific beats glowing — “they found the leak two other companies missed” converts; “great company, highly recommend!!” is furniture. Names, towns, and faces (with permission) multiply believability. And keep them fresh: a newest testimonial from 2019 quietly tells every reader the good times ended.

Questions people actually ask

What's the difference between a review and a testimonial?

Reviews live on platforms you don't control (Google, Yelp) and carry public credibility; testimonials are quotes you've been given permission to use in your own materials. A strong business runs both systems — and both start with asking at the happy moment.

Is it legal to pay customers for testimonials?

Only with clear disclosure — the FTC requires any material connection (payment, discount, free product) to be disclosed conspicuously. Undisclosed paid praise is illegal. The cleaner path: real words from unpaid customers, which also happen to convert better.

Can I edit a customer's testimonial?

Trim for length, fix a typo, cut a tangent — fine. Change the meaning, inflate the claim, or merge words they never said — no. The FTC standard is the endorser's honest experience and opinion; the quote must remain theirs.

How many testimonials do I need?

Two or three specific, current ones per service beat forty generic ones. Coverage matters more than volume: each major service and each common doubt (price, speed, cleanliness, communication) deserves one testimonial that answers it.
