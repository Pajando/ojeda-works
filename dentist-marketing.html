Dentist marketing: the honest playbook.

Nobody shops for a dentist the way they shop for tacos. They're nervous, they're comparing three practices on a Tuesday night, and they pick the one that feels least risky. Dental marketing is trust engineering — everything else is decoration.

Win the comparison you can't see

New patients almost never call the first practice they find; they compare. The profile with 200 specific reviews (“they explained the cost before touching anything”) beats the one with 40 star-only ratings. The site that lists prices for cleanings and answers “do you take my insurance” in plain text beats the one with a smiling stock family. Fill in what a nervous stranger needs to feel safe, and the phone rings.

Recall is the real engine

A practice that keeps patients on six-month recall doesn't need a constant stream of strangers. The unglamorous machinery — reminder texts that sound human, easy rebooking, a front desk that asks for the next appointment at checkout — grows a practice faster than any ad campaign. Marketing to people who already trust you is nearly free; do it first.

The foundation and what to skip

Google Business Profile complete and photographed like a real place, not a brochure. Reviews asked at the happy moment — after the numb wears off, in the patient's own words. A site that answers the actual questions: insurance, financing, emergencies, sedation for the scared. What to skip: buying “dentist near me” ads before the profile can convert them, coupon-site blasts that attract one-visit bargain hunters, and any marketer promising “30 new patients a month” — ask them which 30 and from where.

Questions people actually ask

How do dentists get new patients?

Referrals and search, in that order — and search is a comparison, not a discovery. A complete profile with many specific reviews, honest photos, and a site that answers insurance and cost questions wins the comparison. Ads can add volume only after that foundation converts.

How much should a dental practice spend on marketing?

Common practice runs three to six percent of revenue, more when opening or expanding. But sequence beats budget: recall systems and review momentum cost almost nothing and outperform paid campaigns pointed at a thin profile.

Do Google Ads work for dentists?

They can — dental keywords are expensive, often tens of dollars per click, so the math only works when the profile and site convert those clicks and the practice tracks which calls became patients. Start small, measure to the booked appointment, not the click.

What should a dentist post on social media?

The team, the office, the how-it-works — content that lowers fear. Before-and-afters with written permission. Nobody follows a dental practice for daily content; two real posts a week that make the place feel human is the whole job.
