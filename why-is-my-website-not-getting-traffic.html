Why is my website not getting traffic?

Hard truth first: a website isn't owed traffic. Search engines send visitors to pages that answer what someone typed — and most small-business sites answer nothing. They say “Welcome to our website” in six different ways and wonder why strangers never arrive. The diagnosis has three layers; here they are in the order to check them.

Layer one: can machines read it at all?

Google's Search Essentials lays out the entry requirements: pages have to be findable, crawlable, and readable. The classic small-business failures are mechanical — the site is one big image or a script that renders no text, key info lives only in Instagram posts, no page titles worth clicking, no sitemap, or the whole thing loads so slowly on a phone that visitors leave before it paints. Free check: search Google for site:yourdomain.com. If your pages aren't there, or they're there with meaningless titles, start here — nothing else matters until machines can read you.

Layer two: does any page answer a real question?

Traffic comes from queries. “Home,” “About,” “Services,” “Contact” match zero queries — nobody searches your navigation. People search “how much does a kitchen repaint cost” and “dog groomer in [town] prices,” and the site that answers those questions in plain text gets those visitors. That's the entire content strategy: one page per real question your customers actually ask, written honestly. It's also why we verify every topic against real search data before writing a page — a page targeting a question nobody asks earns exactly nobody. This is the layer where most small-business sites fail completely, and it's the most fixable.

Layer three: does anyone vouch for you?

Between two sites that both answer the question, search engines favor the one the world corroborates: consistent listings, a complete business profile, mentions and links from local organizations, chambers, suppliers, press. Not bought links — real ones, earned the boring way. And increasingly, the same corroboration decides what AI assistants say when someone asks for a recommendation: they cross-check what your site claims against what the rest of the internet says about you. If the layers sound like work — they are, once, and then they compound. It's the work we do.

Questions people actually ask

How long does it take for a new website to get traffic?

Months, not days — even done right. Indexing takes days to weeks; ranking for real queries builds over months as pages prove they answer them. A new site with no question-answering pages can sit at near-zero forever, which is the actual problem to fix.

Why does my website show up for my business name but nothing else?

Because your name is the only question it answers. Ranking for “your name” is automatic; ranking for “services you sell + your town” requires pages that answer those searches in readable text. Each service and town needs its own honest answer.

Do I need to blog every week to get traffic?

No — frequency is not the lever. One page that fully answers a question people actually search beats fifty weekly posts written for a calendar. Verify the question is real, answer it better than what currently ranks, and let it compound.

Is social media traffic a substitute for search traffic?

No — they do different jobs. Social reaches people who weren't looking; search catches people at the moment they need you. A business with followers but no search presence is invisible at exactly the moment the wallet opens.
