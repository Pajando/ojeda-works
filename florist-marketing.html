Florist marketing: the honest playbook.

A florist's demand arrives on a calendar — Valentine's, Mother's Day, prom, wedding season, funerals with no warning at all. The shops that thrive market ahead of the calendar and build the two revenue streams that don't spike: weddings and standing accounts.

Market ahead of the calendar

Every florist knows the big days; few market them early enough. Valentine's content starts in January, Mother's Day in April, prom and graduation in spring — pre-order campaigns with real deadlines (“order by the 10th for guaranteed delivery”) convert the planners and smooth the chaos. Between holidays, the everyday occasions carry the shop: birthdays, apologies, just-because. That's a social content engine — this week's arrangements, shot simply in natural light, posted when they're made.

Weddings and accounts are the ballast

Holiday spikes pay rent; weddings and standing accounts build wealth. Weddings: a portfolio page with real work and honest starting prices, relationships with planners and venues, and responsiveness — couples book vendors who reply the same day. Standing accounts: restaurants, hotels, offices, churches with weekly arrangements — recurring revenue that no algorithm touches. One pitch letter and a sample arrangement to ten local businesses is worth more than a month of boosted posts.

The foundation and what to skip

Google Business Profile with current photos and accurate holiday hours — the pre-holiday “florist near me open now” search is won or lost on profile accuracy. A site with delivery areas, ordering cutoffs, and price ranges in readable text; sympathy buyers especially are stressed strangers who need answers fast, and AI assistants answering “same-day flower delivery [town]” cite whoever states it plainly. What to skip: competing with wire services on their terms — the fees gut margins; instead market direct ordering (“order from our site, not the 800 number — same flowers, better arrangement, we keep the whole price”).

Questions people actually ask

How do florists get more customers?

Freshness made visible — this week's real arrangements on the profile and social — plus early holiday campaigns with order deadlines, and direct-order messaging that pulls buyers away from wire-service middlemen.

How can a florist compete with grocery store flowers?

Don't compete on price; compete on occasion. Groceries sell bunches; florists sell “this will make her cry at her desk.” Design skill, delivery, and message-writing are the product — market the moments, not the stems.

Are weddings worth it for a small flower shop?

Usually yes — high-ticket, bookable months ahead, portfolio-building. The keys: honest starting prices published (filters mismatched inquiries), planner and venue relationships, and same-day inquiry responses during engagement season.

Should florists use wire services like FTD or Teleflora?

Cautiously — order volume comes with fees that gut margins and generic-arrangement expectations. Many shops do better marketing direct ordering locally and keeping wire services as overflow rather than the main pipe.
