Catering business marketing: the honest playbook.

Catering is bought on proof and nerves — someone's wedding, someone's product launch, someone's boss watching. Your marketing exists to lower the buyer's fear: show the food, show the events, answer the cost question before they're embarrassed to ask it.

Show the food at real events

Studio food photos sell restaurants; event photos sell caterers. The buffet line mid-service, plated courses going out, the taco bar at the ranch wedding — proof you deliver at volume in the real world is what event planners scan for. Shoot every event (couples and companies say yes), caption with event type and headcount, and build the portfolio by category: weddings, corporate, quinceañeras, whatever you actually serve. Cuisine specialty is a weapon — “the paella caterer” gets remembered and referred; “full-service catering” gets forgotten.

Corporate accounts are the ballast

Weddings are seasonal peaks; corporate is the weekly baseline — lunch meetings, trainings, holiday parties, the office that orders every Friday. One office manager relationship can be worth a wedding season. Pitch directly: a sample drop-off and a one-page menu to twenty local offices beats a month of ads. Make repeat ordering effortless (standing menus, easy invoicing) and the account renews itself.

The foundation and what to skip

Google Business Profile with event photos and reviews naming occasions. A site with menus, per-person price ranges, service types (drop-off vs full-service — buyers filter on this), and booking lead times in plain text; “catering near me prices” and “how much does catering cost per person” are asked to search engines and AI assistants by every event planner alive, and almost no caterer answers publicly. Answering it wins the citation and the inquiry. What to skip: bidding on lead platforms against six caterers for tire-kickers, and taking every event type badly instead of owning the ones you're great at.

Questions people actually ask

How do catering businesses get more clients?

Event proof by category, per-person pricing stated in ranges, venue and planner relationships, and direct pitches to corporate offices. The caterer who answers the cost question publicly gets the inquiry the silent competitors never see.

Should a caterer publish prices?

Ranges per person by service style, yes. Event buyers budget before they inquire; hiding prices loses the fits and attracts the mismatches. Transparency also makes you the answer when planners ask AI assistants about typical costs.

How important are venue relationships for caterers?

Preferred-vendor lists at venues are a standing referral pipe — earned with flawless events, easy communication, and leaving kitchens cleaner than found. Two venue relationships can fill a wedding season.

What should a catering company post on social media?

Real events in motion: the spread, the service, the load-in time-lapse, the menu of the week. Tag venues and planners (they reshare). The feed should prove volume and polish at once.
