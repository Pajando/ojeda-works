Content & Social

Empty days on your calendar? Handyman marketing that fills them, honestly.

Picture a homeowner who calls a few handymen and waits for anyone to call back. My take: that wait is your real competition, not the other guys. Show up when you said, for the price you said, and people remember. Marketing just makes that easy to see before the first job.

Make reliability visible

The homeowner's fear is specific: no-shows, vague prices, a job left half done. Answer it in public. Ask happy customers to say what actually happened, in their own words, never from a script. A review like “replied in an hour, came Thursday like he said” does more than five stars alone. Add photos of finished jobs and your real name and face. Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking (Google Business Profile Help). Ask right after the fix, while they're still happy about it.

The list is the business

My take: a house that let you in once will need you again, and past clients are the easiest work you'll ever win. Keep a simple list. Check in each season (“Gutter season. Want on the schedule?”). Treat small jobs as a tryout for bigger ones. Property managers and realtors can send repeat work orders too, so be the one who sends photos when the job is done and never needs chasing.

The foundation and what to skip

Start with your Google Business Profile: photos of real work, the services you do (and the ones you don't), the towns you cover, and your license if you have one. Google says businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results (Google Business Profile Help). Then build a one-page site with the same info in plain text, so people, search engines and AI tools can all read it. What to skip: racing to the cheapest hourly rate, and making lead apps your whole plan. My take: you end up paying for strangers while your past clients forget your number. Stay in front of your list instead.

Quick answers

How does a handyman get more clients?

Reviews that prove you show up, a complete Google profile, and steady repeat business. My take: seasonal check-ins with past clients and one or two property managers can keep a calendar full without paying for leads.

Should a handyman charge hourly or by the job?

My take: by the job when the scope is clear, because a flat number is easier for a homeowner to say yes to. Keep hourly for work you truly can't predict. Either way, put the number in writing before you start.

Do handyman apps like TaskRabbit or Thumbtack work?

As a starter or a way to fill gaps, they can help. As the whole business, I wouldn't. You pay the app for the work, and the customer found the app, not you. My take: use them to get your first reviews and start your client list, then let the list take over.

Does a handyman need a website?

One page, yes: services, towns, photos, reviews, license info, and how to reach you, written in plain text that search engines and AI tools can read. My take: without it, people only hear about you by word of mouth.
