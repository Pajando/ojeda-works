Paying for drone footage you can't use? Drone video cost, honestly.

Paid for drone footage you can't use, or worried you'll hire a guy with a drone and no license? Here's what drives the price, the federal rule that separates real operators, and when aerials are worth it.

How drone work is priced

My take, an estimate and not a survey: hourly often runs about $150 to $500 or more, depending on the operator's experience and gear. Per project, think a few hundred dollars for a simple real estate shoot up to a few thousand for produced commercial work with editing. Get two or three written quotes in your area to check this. Editing bills like all video: expect it in the quote or on top of it. Location complexity (airspace, permissions) and deliverables drive the spread.

The license that separates pros

In the US, the FAA's small drone rules, called Part 107, say no one may act as remote pilot in command without a remote pilot certificate. The main way around it is flying strictly for recreational purposes, and filming your business isn't that. So the first question to any drone operator: “Can I see your Part 107 certificate and your insurance?” A real operator will show you both.

When drone shots earn their cost

Aerials sell scale and setting: property exteriors, land, venues, events, anything where the surroundings are the story. They add little when your story happens indoors or face to face. A barbershop needs the chair, not the roof. My take: use drone footage as one ingredient in a video built around one real point, not as the point itself. Establishing shots earn their cost; drone-for-drone's-sake reads as showing off.

Questions people actually ask

How much does drone footage cost for real estate?

My take, an estimate and not a survey: often a few hundred dollars per property for stills and short clips from a licensed operator, sometimes bundled with ground video. Get local quotes. Aerials earn their cost when the lot, the view or the neighborhood is a selling point.

Do drone operators need a license?

In the US, yes, for almost any work flight. Under the FAA Part 107 rules, the pilot in command needs a remote pilot certificate unless the flight is strictly recreational. Always ask to see the certificate and proof of insurance. Real operators expect the question.

Why does drone video cost more than regular video?

Certification, the aircraft and backup gear, insurance, and airspace planning, plus the skill of flying smooth camera moves. You're paying for legal, insured, repeatable results, not just the gadget.

Is drone video worth it for a small business?

My take: if your setting sells (property, venue, land, big outdoor space), yes, as part of a story. If your business happens indoors or one-on-one, spend the same budget on chair-level video that shows the actual work. The honest answer is about your story, not the technology.
