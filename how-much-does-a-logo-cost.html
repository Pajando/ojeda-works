Stuck with a logo you can't put on a sign? Logo cost, honestly.

You paid for a logo, then the sign shop asks for a vector file and you don't have one. My take: logo prices run from a few dollars to tens of thousands, depending on who makes it. Here's what each tier actually buys, and what a small business really needs.

The tiers, honestly

Marketplaces and AI generators: you get a mark, fast. My take: expect roughly $5 to $100, and expect it to look like plenty of others. Read the license, because the rights to stock or generated parts can be unclear.

Freelance designers: my take is a few hundred to a few thousand dollars, and the best fit for most small businesses. You're paying someone to think about your business before drawing, plus proper files and usage rights in writing.

Agencies and brand studios: you're buying research, strategy and a full identity system. My take: expect five figures and up. That can make sense for a big company. For a local business that needs to look trustworthy and get found, it's more than you need.

What actually drives the price

Revisions, research and rights. A cheap logo can skip all three: no questions about your customers, few rounds of changes, and sometimes no clear transfer of ownership. Ask any designer two questions. How many revision rounds are included? Do I own the final files outright, including the vector source? Their answers tell you a lot.

What a small business actually needs

One mark that works small (a phone screen, a map pin) and big (a sign), in color and in one color, delivered as vector files you own. That's it. Brand books can wait. Looking the same everywhere your customers see you matters now. My take: paying a few hundred dollars once beats redoing a cheap logo three times.

Quick answers

Is a cheap AI-generated logo good enough to start?

For testing a business idea, yes. The tradeoffs: it can look generic, the rights to its parts can be unclear, and you may redo it once the business is real. If you go cheap, treat it as a placeholder, not your brand.

What files should I get with a logo?

Vector source files (SVG, AI or EPS), plus PNG exports in color, white and single-color versions. My take: if a designer only hands you a JPG, you bought a picture of a logo, not the logo. Every sign, shirt and truck wrap will be a fight.

How many logo revisions are normal?

My take: two or three planned rounds is a fair deal. An unlimited-revisions offer can mean the price already assumes lots of back-and-forth. A few thoughtful rounds, with a reason behind each change, beat endless tweaks.

Does my logo affect how I show up on Google?

It can show up there. Google lets you add a logo to your Business Profile, and the profile highlights it once basic info like your phone and hours is filled in (Google Business Profile Help). On your website, logo markup can help Google understand which logo you want shown in search results, though Google doesn't promise it will show (Google Search Central). My take: using the same logo everywhere matters more than how pretty it is.
