Gaps in your schedule? How to get clients for a cleaning business, and keep them.

An empty day on your calendar is a day with no pay. Cleaning has a trust problem (you're inside people's homes) and a prize (clients who book every week or two). My take: every good marketing move solves one or the other. Here's the order I'd do it in.

Solve trust before anything

Customers are letting a stranger into their home or office. My take: proof and a fast, friendly first reply do more than any ad. Say plainly that you're insured (and bonded, if you are). Show real photos of real jobs. Ask for a review right after the first clean, while the place still shines, and suggest they mention the service and the town in their own words. Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking (Google Business Profile Help). Put all of it on a complete Google Business Profile so it's there when someone searches “house cleaning near me.”

Chase recurring, not one-offs

Do the math. A client who books every two weeks is 26 cleans a year. A one-time deep clean is one. So make the regular plan the main option you show, and offer the one-time clean as the trial that leads there. Offices and Airbnb hosts can book on a schedule too. My take: a page that speaks straight to them (“office cleaning [town]”) gives that search something to find.

Where paid fits

My take: search ads make the most sense for jobs with a deadline, like “move out cleaning [town],” because that person needs it done this week. Start small and track what each booked job costs you. Before you pay a lead site, ask whether the same lead also goes to other cleaners. Count closed jobs, not leads, before you renew. And use your before-and-after photos everywhere, because the change is what customers are paying for.

Quick answers

How do I get my first cleaning clients?

Start with people who know you. Tell everyone you're launching, clean a few homes at fair prices, and ask each happy client for a review and a referral. My take: a handful of reviews with real details plus a complete Google profile gets you started. After that it's good work and asking every time.

How much should I charge for cleaning?

Price from your market and your real costs. Look at the rates other local cleaners post, then count your hours, supplies, insurance and drive time. My take: pricing low just to win clients tends to bring in the ones who leave the moment someone is cheaper. Regular clients are paying for someone who shows up every time, so charge a fair rate for it.

Are cleaning lead sites like Thumbtack worth it?

Sometimes, to fill gaps while you build your own profile. Before you pay, ask whether the same lead goes to other cleaners too. Then track what a closed regular client costs you there versus from your Google profile and referrals, and put your money where the clients actually come from.

What insurance do cleaning businesses need to advertise?

The SBA says laws requiring insurance vary by state, and suggests talking with insurance agents about what coverage you need (SBA). General liability covers things like property damage and injury claims. Ask the agent about bonding too. Then, if it's true, say “insured and bonded” plainly on your profile and site. My take: it answers a homeowner's biggest worry before they have to ask.
