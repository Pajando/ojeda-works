Hungry people skipping your menu? Restaurant menu design that's easy to read and easy to find.

A menu is a sales tool read by hungry people in a hurry. It is also read by Google and AI apps deciding who to recommend. Good design works for both. Here's what holds up and what's myth.

Readability beats decoration

People scan menus, they don't read them. Use clear sections, dish names that say what the dish is, short descriptions that make people hungry, and prices that are easy to find. My take: hiding prices costs you more trust than it earns. Put your best-loved, best-margin dishes where they are easy to spot, like the top of a section or in a box. In my view, a lot of the “menu engineering” tricks sold online are more show than science.

Pricing presentation, honestly

My take: dropping dollar signs and the .99 endings looks cleaner, and that's fine. Burying prices inside paragraphs makes regulars trust you less. One higher-priced dish can make the rest look fair, but every item should earn its spot. A short menu of dishes you're proud of is easier on the kitchen and faster for the customer to choose from.

The digital menu is the one machines read

Your menu lives twice: on paper at the table, and online for the person deciding where to eat. Google can index PDF files (Google's list of file types it can index), but a PDF is slow to pinch and zoom on a phone. My take: real text on the page is the safest way to make sure Google and AI apps can read your dishes when someone searches “best mole in [town],” and a photo of a menu is the worst way. Put the same menu on your Google profile and update it when prices change. An old online menu with wrong prices hurts more than it helps.

Questions people actually ask

Should my menu be a PDF on my website?

No. Google can read PDF files, but a PDF is slow to open and zoom on a phone. My take: put the menu on the page as real text, with sections and prices. Keep the PDF for the print shop. The web copy is the one that helps you get found.

How many items should a restaurant menu have?

My take: fewer than you think. A shorter menu makes choosing faster, keeps the kitchen moving, and needs less stock. If everything is a specialty, nothing is. Cut what you wouldn't proudly recommend to a first-timer.

Do menu design tricks really increase sales?

My take: the small, honest ones can help. Put favorites where they are easy to spot, keep prices clean, and anchor honestly. Anyone promising a big sales jump from layout alone is overselling. A clear description of a dish the kitchen makes well beats any trick. Design helps the food; it can't replace it.

How much does menu design cost?

My take: prices vary a lot by designer and city, so get two or three quotes. Template tools cost less, but your menu will look like everyone else's. Whatever you print, also do the free half: put the same menu as real text on your site and your Google profile.
