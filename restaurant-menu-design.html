Restaurant menu design: what actually works.

A menu is a sales document read by hungry, impatient people — and increasingly by machines deciding whether to recommend you. Good design serves both readers. Here's what's real and what's myth.

Readability beats decoration

Diners scan, they don't read: clear sections, dish names that say what the dish is, short appetite-driven descriptions, prices aligned honestly (hiding them annoys more than it upsells). The layout psychology that holds up: eyes land first on the upper area and on boxed or highlighted items — put your best-margin, best-loved dishes there. The stuff that doesn't: most “menu engineering” mystique is decoration wearing a lab coat.

Pricing presentation, honestly

Dropping dollar signs and trailing .99s reads cleaner and slightly softens price focus — fine. Deliberately burying prices or nesting them in paragraphs erodes trust with the exact customers you want returning. Anchor honestly: a premium dish makes neighbors look reasonable, but every item should earn its place — a shorter menu of dishes you're proud of outsells a long one that slows the kitchen and the decision.

The digital menu is the one machines read

Your menu lives twice: printed for the table, and on your site for the deciding searcher — and the second copy must be real text, not a PDF or photo. Search engines and AI assistants answering “best mole in [town]” can only recommend dishes they can read. Same text on your Google profile, updated when prices change; a stale online menu costs more goodwill than no menu at all.

Questions people actually ask

Should my menu be a PDF on my website?

No — PDFs are slow on phones and nearly invisible to search engines and AI assistants. Real text on the page, sections and prices included. Keep the PDF for the print shop; the web copy is the one that gets you found and recommended.

How many items should a restaurant menu have?

Fewer than instinct says: a focused menu speeds decisions, kitchens, and inventory, and signals confidence. If everything's a specialty, nothing is. Trim what you wouldn't proudly recommend to a first-timer — the menu is a curation, not an archive.

Do menu design tricks really increase sales?

The modest, real ones: placement of high-margin favorites where eyes land, clean price presentation, honest anchoring. The mystical ones oversell. Nothing outsells a clearly described dish the kitchen executes well — design amplifies the food, never replaces it.

How much does menu design cost?

A freelance designer commonly charges a few hundred dollars for a clean print menu; template tools cost less with genericness as the tradeoff. Pair whatever you print with the free half most restaurants skip: the same menu as readable text on your site and profile.
