Daycare marketing: the honest playbook.

No parent picks a daycare from an ad — they pick from a gut feeling on the tour, after weeks of reading everything findable about you. Daycare marketing has one job: earn the tour. Everything else is the tour's supporting evidence.

Everything is a trust signal

Parents research daycares like investigators: license status, ratios, staff tenure, reviews, photos of the actual rooms. Put it all in public — license number, ratios by age group, teacher bios with real tenure, daily-schedule samples, meal policy. The center that answers every anxious question before the phone call gets the tour request; the one that says “call for details” reads as hiding something. Reviews from current families, mentioning teachers by name, are the strongest signal there is.

The tour is the close — engineer the path to it

Make requesting a tour effortless: a form with real available times, same-day response, confirmation that sounds like a human. During the tour, the marketing is the place itself; after, a warm follow-up with the enrollment steps and honest waitlist status. Speaking of which: a waitlist is marketing gold when handled honestly — “we're full for infants until June, here's how the list works” signals demand and fairness at once.

The foundation and what to skip

Google Business Profile complete and current — hours, photos of rooms and outdoor space, every review answered warmly. A site with programs, ages, ratios, sample day, tuition ranges, and enrollment process in plain text; “daycare near me with prices” and “how much is daycare” are asked to search engines and AI assistants by every expecting parent. What to skip: stock photos of children who don't attend (parents notice and it reads false), discount enrollment promos (price-signaling backfires when the product is trust), and any claim you can't back on the tour.

Questions people actually ask

How do daycares attract new families?

Referrals from current families and search, both won by public trust signals: license and ratio transparency, teacher tenure, current-family reviews, and an effortless tour-request path with fast response.

Should a daycare list tuition online?

Ranges, yes. Parents filter by budget before touring, and hiding prices loses the families who would have fit while attracting awkward mismatched tours. Transparency also gets you cited when parents ask AI assistants about local costs.

What should a daycare post on social media?

The place and the programs — art projects, the garden, teacher spotlights — with strict photo-permission discipline for children. Consistency and warmth matter more than frequency; two real posts a week is plenty.

How does a daycare handle a waitlist without losing families?

Honestly and systematically: state current availability by age group, explain how the list moves, and check in when spots approach. Families plan months ahead — a transparent waitlist converts better than a vague “call us.”
