Contractor marketing: the honest playbook.

Contractors have a marketing problem nobody names: customers are scared of hiring the wrong one. Every effective contractor marketing move is really a trust move. Here's the playbook, no retainer required.

Solve the trust problem first

Homeowners aren't shopping for a contractor; they're trying not to get burned. So the assets that convert are proof: job photos with real befores-and-afters, reviews that mention the job type and town, license and insurance stated plainly, and a real human answering. Your Google Business Profile carrying all four beats any ad campaign pointed at a thin one.

The free foundation, contractor edition

Complete profile with your service area, job-type photos organized by category, and steady honest reviews — ask at the final walkthrough, when the homeowner is happiest, and suggest they mention the job and the town in their own words. That wording is exactly what Google and AI assistants use to match you to “kitchen remodel [town]” searches. A simple site listing services, areas, and proof rounds it out; machines can't recommend what they can't read.

Where paid fits (and the marketing-agency pitch)

Search ads on urgent-need trades (plumbing, electrical, roofing after storms) convert because intent is immediate — start small, track cost per real lead. Lead-selling platforms deserve skepticism: you're bidding against four others for the same shared lead. And the contractor-marketing agencies that fill your feed sell retainers for what's mostly the free foundation above — read their deliverables list before signing anything.

Questions people actually ask

What's the best marketing for a small contractor?

Job photos plus reviews plus a complete profile — the trust trio. It's free, it compounds, and it's what homeowners actually check before calling. Every paid channel works better after it's in place and worse instead of it.

Are lead generation sites worth it for contractors?

Sometimes as filler, rarely as foundation: shared leads mean bidding wars, and quality varies wildly. Track what a *closed job* costs you through them versus through your own profile and referrals — most contractors who measure end up reinvesting in their own presence.

How do contractors get good reviews?

Ask at the final walkthrough — the happiest moment — and make it one tap via the review link. Suggest mentioning the job type and town in their own words, never a script. Reviews naming “deck rebuild in [town]” are the ones that win you the next deck rebuild in that town.

Do contractors need a website if they have plenty of work?

Busy today isn't found tomorrow — referral pipelines age. A simple site (services, areas, proof, license info) is cheap insurance that keeps you visible to searches and AI recommendations for the season when the phone slows. Build it while you're busy, not after.
