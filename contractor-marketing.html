Homeowners hiring someone else? Contractor marketing that earns the call, honestly.

You bid the job. They hired someone else. My take: homeowners are scared of hiring the wrong contractor, so they pick the one who looks safest. That makes good contractor marketing mostly about trust. Here's how to build it without a monthly agency bill.

Solve the trust problem first

My take: homeowners aren't shopping for a contractor. They're trying not to get burned. So show them proof: real before-and-after job photos, reviews that name the job and the town, your license and insurance stated plainly, and a real person who picks up the phone. Put all four on your Google Business Profile before you spend a dollar on ads that send people to it.

The free foundation, contractor edition

Fill out your profile completely, including your service area. Add job photos sorted by type of work. Then get steady, honest reviews. Ask at the final walkthrough, when the homeowner can see the finished job, and suggest they mention the job and the town in their own words. Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking (Google Business Profile Help). My take: a review that says “kitchen remodel in [town]” also tells the next homeowner, and any AI tool reading it, what you do and where. Last, add a simple website with your services, the towns you cover, and your proof. If it isn't written down anywhere, search tools have nothing to go on.

Where paid fits (and the marketing-agency pitch)

My take: search ads make the most sense for urgent jobs like a burst pipe, a dead electrical panel, or a roof after a storm, because that person needs help today. Start small and track what each real lead costs you. Be careful with sites that sell leads. A shared lead goes to more than one contractor, so you can end up competing on price. And before you sign with a contractor-marketing agency, read the list of what you get each month. I think a lot of it is the free work above.

Quick answers

What's the best marketing for a small contractor?

My take: job photos, reviews and a complete Google profile. They cost nothing but time, they keep working after you post them, and they're what you want a homeowner to see before they call. Paid ads work better on top of them, not instead of them.

Are lead generation sites worth it for contractors?

Sometimes, to fill gaps. I wouldn't build a business on them. A shared lead goes to more than one contractor, so you can end up competing on price. Track what a closed job costs you from lead sites versus from your own profile and referrals, then put your money where the jobs actually come from.

How do contractors get good reviews?

Ask at the final walkthrough, when the homeowner can see the finished job, and send your review link so it's one tap. Suggest they mention the job and the town in their own words. Never hand them a script. My take: a review that says “deck rebuild in [town]” is the kind that helps the next homeowner in that town pick you.

Do contractors need a website if they have plenty of work?

My take: yes. Referrals slow down when past clients move away or forget your name. A simple site with your services, the towns you cover, job photos and license info is cheap insurance. It gives Google and AI tools something to read about you before the phone gets quiet. Build it while you're busy, not after.
