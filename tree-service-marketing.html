Losing tree jobs to cheaper crews? Tree service marketing that earns the call, honestly.

Tree work is costly, risky, and not something people buy often, so your customer is usually a nervous homeowner comparing strangers with chainsaws near their house. My take: the company that proves insurance, skill, and cleanup before the estimate has the best shot at the job, often without being the lowest bid.

Credentials are the marketing

Insured, and provably: certificate available on request, stated on the site, mentioned in the profile. Certified arborist on staff? Put it in the headline, not a footnote. Someone searching for a certified arborist needs to see those words on your page. Photos of real crews in real safety gear doing real rigging tell a nervous homeowner more than any slogan. My take: a time-lapse of a crane job is one of the strongest ads you can make, and it costs nothing but a phone.

Be findable before the storm

Storm damage brings the most urgent calls in this trade, and you get ready for them before the storm. Google says “Businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results” (Google Business Profile Help). My take: the company with the complete profile, the reviews, and an “emergency tree service” page already on Google is the one that gets the 6am call when the oak is on the garage. Off-season, sell prevention honestly: assessments, cabling, structural pruning before winter. A review that says “they told us which trees could wait” builds trust for a long time.

The foundation and what to skip

Fill your profile with before-and-after removals, stump grinds, and cleanup shots. A yard that looks better after than before is the proof that closes. Your site needs services, service area, insurance, and honest price ranges in plain text. People search “how much does tree removal cost,” and a page that answers it gives them a reason to call you. What to skip: knocking on doors after storms (my take: it looks like the storm chasers homeowners worry about, so let your marketing make you the safe choice), lowballing against uninsured crews (say plainly why the quotes differ), and any job promised without a written scope.

Questions people actually ask

How do tree services get more customers?

Proof of insurance and competence where the comparison happens: a complete profile, reviews mentioning safety and cleanup, arborist credentials stated plainly, and an emergency page indexed before storm season.

Should a tree service list prices online?

Ranges, yes: removal by size and access, trimming, and stump grinding, plus what moves the number. My take: ranges screen out people who only want the cheapest bid, calm nervous buyers, and answer the exact question people type into search engines and AI assistants.

How can a tree service compete with cheaper uninsured crews?

Say it in writing: part of the price difference is often insurance. If an uninsured worker gets hurt on the property, the homeowner may be the one stuck with the bills, so homeowners should ask their own insurance agent or attorney how that works where they live. “Ask every bidder for their certificate of insurance” is a fair line to put on your site.

What should a tree company post on social media?

Big removals, crane and rigging work, time-lapses, stump-to-lawn makeovers, and crew safety habits. This trade makes dramatic video on its own, so post it and let the skill speak.
