How to ask customers for reviews — without being weird about it.

Most businesses don't have a review problem; they have an asking problem. The happy customers walk out the door unrecorded, and the one angry customer finds the review box all by himself. Asking is completely allowed — Google's own help pages tell you to do it. Here's how to do it right, and the two rules that get businesses in real trouble.

The rules, from Google itself

Google's official guidance says plainly: remind customers to leave reviews, share your review link, use a QR code. What it prohibits, in its own words, is “offering incentives, like free or discounted goods or services, in exchange for customers to post reviews” — that's classified as fake and misleading content, and it can get a profile restricted. The other trap is review gating: screening customers first and steering only the happy ones toward Google. Ask everyone, the same way, and let the record be real — that's also what makes it trustworthy to the humans and AI assistants reading it later.

The moment matters more than the words

Ask at the peak: the day the project delivers, the moment they say “this looks great,” the visit where the pain is gone. A week later the glow has faded and the ask feels like homework. Make it effortless — the direct review link by text while they're standing there, or a QR code at the counter — because every extra step loses half the people who meant to do it. And ask in person when you can: “it genuinely helps a small business — would you write a couple of sentences about what we did?” converts better than any automated email sequence.

Coach the review without scripting it

A wall of five-star “Great service!” reviews is nearly useless — to future customers and to the AI assistants that now summarize businesses. What works is detail in the customer's own voice. So ask for it, honestly: “if you can, mention what we actually did and which town you're in — it helps people find us.” Never write it for them, never dictate wording — scripted reviews read as scripted, and they're the customer's words to choose. One specific review (“rewired our 1960s Napa kitchen in two days, spotless cleanup”) outweighs ten generic ones, because it's the only kind that answers a future customer's actual question.

Questions people actually ask

Is it against the rules to ask customers for Google reviews?

No — Google's own help pages encourage reminding customers and sharing your review link. What's prohibited is paying or discounting in exchange for reviews, and filtering so only happy customers get asked. Ask everyone, openly.

Can I offer a discount for leaving a review?

No. Google explicitly classifies incentivized reviews as fake and misleading content, and it can get your profile restricted. The FTC takes the same view when the incentive isn't disclosed. The honest ask works — use it.

What is the best way to ask for a review?

In person, at the happy moment, with a direct link or QR code that removes every step. A short, human ask — including a nudge to mention the specific service and town — beats automated blasts on both response rate and review quality.

What should I do about a bad review?

Reply like an owner who cares — future customers read your replies as a preview of how you handle problems. Never pay, pressure, or trade favors to remove it; bury it instead under a steady stream of real, asked-for reviews.
